Origins and Mechanism

Ollas date back at least 4,000 years, with evidence in North Africa, Han Dynasty China (1st century BCE texts), and widespread Indigenous use across the Americas. 
The Spanish term "olla" (pot) gave the method its modern name, but the principle is timeless: 
an unglazed, porous terracotta vessel buried near plant roots, filled with water, and sealed at the top.Water seeps slowly through the clay walls into the surrounding soil via osmosis and soil moisture tension. 
As roots draw moisture, a consistent micro-zone of field capacity forms around the pot. 
When soil dries, tension increases and release accelerates; when soil is moist, seepage slows or stops.
 Plants effectively “pull” water as needed - no pumps, no timers, no surface evaporation, no runoff.Field trials demonstrate 60–90% water savings compared to flood or surface irrigation, with some studies showing up to 97% reduction for crops like maize and tomatoes. 
Ollas reduce weeds (no surface moisture), foliar disease (no wet leaves), and salinity buildup (targeted root-zone delivery). 
Roots often grow toward and encircle the olla, creating an efficient, direct uptake path.Integration into the Ark's Living SystemOllas are not an add-on; they are a natural extension of the Ark's existing flows and ethics. 
They require no electricity, no plastic, no moving parts 
 only clay, gravity, and biology. They amplify the no-kill, closed-loop, yield-before-snap design we have built.

Riparian Duck Paddies & Rice Zones
Small ollas (1–2 gallon unglazed terracotta pots or DIY glued pairs) are buried every 2–4 feet along paddy edges or between rice plants. Refilling occurs via gravity-fed overflow from braided Delta channels or central basin. Ducks and geese forage around them; manure seeps into the pot zone for nutrient enrichment.
Benefit: 
Consistent root-zone moisture for rice without flooding entire paddies → lowers methane emissions, stabilizes yields in dry periods, and reduces labor while maintaining the rice–duck–fish triad.

Medicinal Mid-Story & Transitional Guilds
Medium ollas (3–5 gallon) are placed near high-value plants (elderberry, echinacea, turmeric, ashwagandha, gotu kola, brahmi). 
Buried at root depth (12–18 inches), with necks exposed for occasional manual or capillary top-up from braided channels.
Benefit: Targeted moisture for drought-sensitive medicinals, reduced foliar disease, enhanced microbiome activity in the root zone.

Upland Orchard Ring & Mature Canopy
Larger ollas (5–10 gallon) are buried near tree bases (figs, citrus, chestnuts, serviceberry, mulberry).
 Integrated with subsurface root cassettes (biochar/mycorrhizae enhance wicking and microbial connectivity).
Benefit: Deep-root watering for canopy trees, minimized surface evaporation loss, long-term soil carbon support through consistent moisture and root exudate cycling.

Halo Buffer & Wetland Edges
Small ollas in outer filtration bands (near cattails, papyrus, pickerelweed) maintain consistent moisture for biofilter plants.
Benefit: Stabilized wetland edges, enhanced amphibian/dragonfly habitat, improved nutrient capture before external exchange.

DIY & Scale Implementation  Construction: Two unglazed terracotta pots glued rim-to-rim (natural clay slip or food-grade silicone), or pot + saucer. 
Lid (stone or saucer) reduces evaporation. 
Cost: <$5 per unit.  
Scale: Begin in the seed hub, replicate fractally across modules. 
Use Delta overflows for automatic gravity-fed top-up via small wicks or channels.  
Maintenance: Refill every 7–14 days (AI soil moisture sensors signal when needed). Periodic cleaning prevents algae.  
Enhancements: Biochar inside pots for slow nutrient release and microbial habitat.
 Mycelial networks connect ollas to roots for distributed efficiency.
