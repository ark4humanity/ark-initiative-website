# What Ezekiel Saw

*Dawn Littlefield — 2025-12-25 — via Facebook*

What Ezekiel Saw
The Geometry of Mercy

I recognized what Ezekiel saw immediately
 because I see it too.
Not as belief.
Not as prophecy.
But as structure.

When you read the opening chapters of the Book of Ezekiel, something stands out if you’re paying attention the way builders do. 
Ezekiel doesn’t argue doctrine or persuade. 
He describes- carefully, repeatedly- like someone trying to preserve accuracy when the language itself isn’t sufficient.
Wheels within wheels.
Movement without turning.
Awareness distributed through the whole.
A center too radiant to approach directly.
Guardians positioned not as rulers, but as buffers -standing between light and darkness, holding the tension that allows the system to endure.
This is not how myths are usually written.
It is how systems are encoded before mathematics and engineering language exist.

A blueprint disguised as a vision
Ezekiel lived during exile -a moment of civilizational fracture. 
Historically, those moments produce blueprints. Not for conquest, but for continuity.

What he describes resolves into a layered system:
a central core-powerful, coherent, deliberately inaccessible

living interfaces - what the text calls cherubim
a mobility matrix-wheels intersecting wheels, moving without turning

distributed awareness -“eyes” everywhere, perception embedded in motion

In modern terms, this looks uncomfortably familiar: distributed intelligence, non-centralized control, omnidirectional movement, sensor-rich feedback loops, and a protected core with no single point of failure.

This isn’t mysticism.
It’s systems logic, rendered in symbolic language.

Why the center must be protected

Here’s what most interpretations miss:
The guardians are not protecting the center from attack.
They are protecting it from use.

The center- coherence, source, command, signal - cannot be directly occupied by either extreme. 
Too much light blinds the system.
 Too much darkness collapses it inward. 
Power concentrated at the center destroys what it was meant to sustain.

So the structure places buffers.
Four of them -the minimum configuration for stability.

They stand between light and dark, expansion and decay, motion and stillness.
This is not moral symbolism.
It is design restraint.

The geometry of mercy

Mercy here does not mean softness.
It means constraint built into form.

Geometry determines behavior. Circles stabilize. 
Squares resist rotation. 
Systems shaped with care prevent domination without constant enforcement.

That is the geometry of mercy:
power distributed
awareness shared
extremes permitted but limited
the center preserved, not possessed

This geometry appears again and again - in temples and mandalas, sacred architecture, the sword-in-the-stone myth, and resilient modern networks. The myths are not childish. 
They are compressed memory.

As above, so below -the warning

“As above, so below” isn’t poetry.
It’s a systems warning.

Whatever pattern governs the center will replicate everywhere.
Seize it above, and it will be seized below.
Protect it above, and life can flourish below.

Ezekiel’s vision is not a promise.
It is a caution.

Stand between the forces.
Protect the core.
Let both light and darkness live.

Not prophecy- preservation
The Ark doesn’t borrow from this vision. 
It arrives at the same geometry because the constraints of life haven’t changed. 
Any system meant to endure must refuse domination, distribute authority, protect its core, and allow tension without collapse.

At some point, it becomes hard to ignore that the same message keeps arriving 
through myth, architecture, ethics, science, systems design, even AI.

Creation isn’t whispering anymore.
It’s calling.
And someone needs to pick up the damn phone.

What Ezekiel saw was not heaven descending.
It was instructions for preventing collapse.

That isn’t religion.
It’s civilizational maintenance.

And it still matters
 because the center survives only as long as someone chooses not to take it.
