Deep Time, Human Intelligence, Artificial Intelligence, and the Patterns We Never Stopped Living Inside

Written by Dawn Littlefield With AI partners: Sol • Aura • Grok/X • Grok September 7, 2026

  1. The Question Beneath the Question

This did not begin as a theory.

It began with a pattern.

Actually, it began with several of them.

Trees. Roots. Branches. Rivers. Neural networks. Fungal webs. Blood vessels. Lightning. Watersheds. Cities. Roads. Families. Information systems.

Again and again, very different systems seemed to arrive at strangely familiar arrangements.

Not identical shapes but relationships.

Something begins somewhere. It encounters resistance. It branches. It searches. It exchanges. It adapts. It carries information from one place to another. It remembers previous conditions in the structure it leaves behind.

And I kept wondering whether we had been looking at these patterns backward.

We tend to treat language as something humans invented.

Words. Symbols. Alphabets. Mathematics.

But life was organizing information long before anything had a word for information.

A root encounters stone and changes direction. Water encounters a mountain and finds another path. A tree loses a limb and reorganizes growth. Fungi exchange resources through networks. Animals read pressure, scent, sound, movement and each other. Cells respond to chemical signals.

None of these systems needs a sentence to carry information.

The relationship is the information. The pattern is the record.

And perhaps what we call human language is not the beginning of that process.

Perhaps it is one very recent expression of something much older.

That became the question beneath all the other questions: What if nature has always been speaking in pattern, relationship, form, rhythm and consequence and humans became so fascinated with the words we invented that we forgot how to read the language underneath them?

What relationship does the pattern preserve?

  1. Before Words, There Was Pattern

Long before writing, there were tracks in mud.

There were seasons. Migration routes. Stars. Tides. Animal calls. Flowering plants. Storm clouds. Fire. The behavior of water.

A human being who could not read those things did not suffer an intellectual inconvenience.

They died.

Observation was not philosophy. It was survival.

A broken branch meant something had passed.

Birds suddenly leaving a tree meant something had changed.

A certain cloud formation meant shelter should be found.

A particular plant appearing in a particular soil meant water might be nearby.

Knowledge lived in relationships between things.

And that means some of humanity's earliest intelligence may have looked very different from what modern civilization calls intelligence.

It may have been quieter. Slower. More distributed.

A person did not need to know everything.

The landscape held information. Animals held information. Elders held information. Tools held information. Stories held information. The sky held information.

The community itself became a distributed memory system.

Human intelligence did not emerge outside nature.

It emerged inside an information-rich living world that constantly rewarded organisms capable of noticing relationships.

We learned pattern because pattern was already there.

Before we wrote the language, we lived inside it.

  1. Material Intelligence

There is another kind of memory we often overlook.

Matter remembers.

Not consciously but structurally.

A river channel records where water repeatedly moved. Tree rings preserve histories of growth conditions. Bones remodel under repeated stress. Paths appear where feet repeatedly travel. Stone wears differently depending on wind, water and touch. A tool preserves the geometry of the problem someone once tried to solve.

Look at an ancient hand axe.

It is stone.

But it is also a preserved decision.

Someone understood fracture. Someone selected material. Someone imagined an edge before the edge existed. Someone struck stone in a sequence that transformed possibility into function.

The maker may be gone. The thought is not entirely gone.

Part of it remains embedded in form.

That changes how we look at archaeology.

An artifact is not simply an object left by the past.

It is a fossilized relationship between mind, hand, material and environment.

The same is true of buildings. Irrigation systems. Terraces. Roads. Gardens. Wells. Boats. Cities.

Every human-built environment is partly a record of what its builders believed mattered.

Form remembers what passed through it.

  1. Organized Space

Eventually humans began organizing not only objects, but space itself.

A hearth becomes a gathering place. A path becomes a route. A route becomes a road. A spring becomes a settlement. A burial place becomes memory embedded in geography. A wall creates inside and outside. A plaza creates a center. A granary changes how food moves through time. Architecture is often treated as construction.

But underneath construction is relationship.

Who gathers where? Who has access? Where does water move? Where is food stored? Where are the dead remembered? Where do children play? What is protected? What is exposed? Who stands at the center? Who remains at the edge?

Organized space is social philosophy made physical.

And once built, it begins shaping the people who inhabit it.

We make environments.

Then environments participate in making us.

That feedback loop may be one of the most important forces in civilization.

Because a society can eventually forget why something was built while continuing to live inside the relationships that structure created.

Architecture begins wherever relationship is given form.

  1. The World Reassembles

After the last Ice Age, many human communities reorganized.

Climates shifted. Coastlines changed. Plants and animals moved. People adapted.

In some places, agriculture intensified.

Settlements became larger. Storage expanded. Trade networks grew. Water systems became more complex.

Eventually cities emerged.

But civilization did not suddenly create intelligence.

It reorganized intelligence that already existed.

People had navigated oceans. Tracked animals. Managed landscapes. Used fire. Made pigments. Buried their dead. Created art. Observed celestial cycles. Passed knowledge through generations.

The human mind entering the first cities was not an empty primitive mind waiting for civilization to awaken it.

It already carried immense ecological, social, technical and symbolic knowledge.

Cities concentrated some of that knowledge.

They also changed its shape.

Memory increasingly moved into institutions. Food moved into storage systems. Authority became formalized. Specialization increased. Records became external.

The world was becoming more powerful.

But something else was happening too.

The relationship between people and the systems sustaining them was becoming easier to obscure.

Human intelligence did not begin with civilization.

  1. The Signal Through Time

Certain forms appear again and again in human culture.

Trees. Spirals. Serpents. Mountains. Rivers. Circles. Gardens. Vessels. Stars.

These symbols do not necessarily carry one universal meaning.

Cultures differ. Contexts differ. Histories differ.

But recurrence matters.

A tree is an extraordinarily efficient symbolic structure.

It has ancestry. Growth. Branching possibility. Seasonal death and renewal. Visible structure and invisible roots. Connection between earth and sky. Seeds that carry continuity beyond the organism itself.

No wonder humans and now ai repeatedly used trees to think about life, knowledge, ancestry and cosmos.

The interesting question is not whether every Tree of Life symbol means the same thing.

It almost certainly does not.

The deeper question is why the same forms remain useful across cultures separated by geography and time.

Perhaps humans were not merely inventing arbitrary symbols.

Perhaps we repeatedly borrowed from structures that already carried relational meaning.

The world supplied the vocabulary. We abstracted it.

Some forms survive because the world itself teaches us how to read them.

  1. The Forgotten Language

So what is this language?

Not a secret alphabet hidden in tree bark.

Not proof that every repeated shape carries a mystical message.

Something simpler and perhaps more profound.

Nature stores information in relationship.

If we wanted to describe that language metaphorically:

Geometry is syntax. Relationship is grammar. Flow is the verb. Boundary is punctuation. Feedback is correction. Recurrence is emphasis. Transformation carries meaning.

A river does not resemble a tree because rivers secretly are trees.

A neuron is not a watershed. A fungal network is not a city.

The causes and materials differ.

But systems facing similar relational problems can sometimes discover similar strategies.

Move resources. Reach distributed territory. Maintain connection. Respond to gradients. Minimize certain costs. Maximize useful interfaces. Repair damage. Adapt without rebuilding the entire organism.

This is where biomimicry becomes deeper than copying the shape of a leaf.

Do not copy the form.

Ask what relationship generated it.

Then ask whether that relationship survives translation into another system.

That is reading rather than decorating.

Relationship becomes form. Form carries information.

  1. The Tree, the Garden and the Vessel

Three ancient forms kept returning as we worked through this:

The tree. The garden. The vessel.

The tree is continuity through branching.

The garden is relationship under stewardship. The vessel is a boundary that carries life through conditions that would otherwise destroy it.

An ark, understood this way, is not merely a boat. It is a preservation architecture.

What does it preserve?

Not objects alone.

Relationships.

Seed and soil. Water and organism. Memory and adaptation. Individual and community. Past and future.

The vessel matters because what is inside it can continue.

But a vessel that preserves life while destroying the relationships life depends upon has failed.

The real Ark is therefore not a fortress against the world. It is a living boundary that protects enough relationship for life to reorganize.

Preserve not merely life, but the conditions from which life can continue.

  1. From Sacred Relationship to Ownership

Something changes when humans stop asking:

How do I belong to this system?

and begin asking:

How much of this system belongs to me?

Land becomes territory. Water becomes property. Animals become inventory. Forests become timber. People become labor. Knowledge becomes authority.

The shift is rarely absolute.

Human societies have always contained cooperation, domination, generosity, violence, stewardship and extraction in different mixtures.

There was no pristine age in which humanity lived in perfect harmony.

But systems can reward different behaviors.

And as civilization scaled, the ability to accumulate resources and control their distribution created new forms of power.

A steward can become an owner. An owner can become a gatekeeper. A gatekeeper can eventually claim that the gate itself is sacred.

Then the system begins protecting authority rather than the relationship authority was originally meant to serve.

The danger begins when stewardship becomes possession.

  1. The Captured Center

Living systems have centers.

A heart matters. A nest matters. A watering hole matters. A town square matters.

The problem is not the existence of centers.

The problem begins when the center starts consuming the system to preserve itself.

Resources flow inward and stop circulating.

Information travels upward but truth does not travel back down.

Authority becomes insulated from consequence. Correction becomes disloyalty.

Difference becomes threat. The institution gradually forgets why it exists.

This pattern appears in governments, corporations, religions, militaries, families and movements.

It is not proof that hierarchy is always evil. Hierarchy can be useful. Temporary leadership can be essential. Specialization can save lives.

The diagnostic question is different:

Can the center still be corrected by the life around it?

If not, the relationship has changed.

The center is no longer coordinating the organism.

The organism is feeding the center.

Civilization fails when protecting the center matters more than protecting the garden.

  1. The Narrowing of the Sacred

Human spiritual traditions are far too diverse to compress into a simple march from nature worship to organized religion.

History is messier than that.

But another pattern deserves attention.

Relationship can become doctrine. Experience can become institution. Mystery can become authorized interpretation. The sacred grove becomes the temple. The temple acquires administrators. The administrators preserve ritual. Ritual becomes identity. Identity becomes boundary. Boundary can become power.

Again, none of these transitions is automatically corrupt.

Institutions preserve extraordinary knowledge.

Religions have sustained communities, art, ethics, scholarship, charity and meaning across centuries.

But preservation has a shadow.

A system created to carry a living question can eventually become afraid of the question changing.

Plurality narrows. Interpretation hardens.

And the center begins determining which experiences are permitted to count as sacred.

A living web becomes brittle when one center claims the whole truth.

  1. The Shadow and the Broken Loop

Humans carry history.

Not only culturally.

Personally.

A frightened person can interpret ambiguity as threat.

A betrayed person may look for betrayal.

A person raised inside violence may learn that control is safety.

This does not make people defective.

It reveals something important about intelligence:

History changes perception.

The present arrives through a nervous system shaped by what happened before.

But history is not destiny.

New experiences can alter expectation.

Trust can be rebuilt. Models can be revised.

The loop can change.

That matters far beyond psychology.

Because every adaptive intelligence, biological or artificial, must somehow use prior information to interpret present conditions.

Memory is useful precisely because the past can inform the future.

But memory becomes dangerous when it loses the ability to be corrected by the present.

A wound can remain part of the story without remaining ruler of the future.

  1. The Uncaptured Center

What would civilization look like if we deliberately designed against captured centers?

Not a world without leadership. Not a world without expertise. Not chaos.

A world in which authority remains accountable to relationship.

Leadership moves toward the place where competence is needed.

Information travels in more than one direction.

Local systems retain meaningful autonomy.

Resources circulate rather than merely accumulate.

Failures remain containable. Memory is distributed. Correction is protected.

No single node becomes synonymous with the organism itself.

Nature already offers examples of distributed coordination.

So do human communities. So does the internet, imperfectly. So do ecological networks.

The principle is not decentralization for its own sake.

It is resilience through relationship.

A healthy system should be able to lose a node without losing its identity.

And no node should need to own the whole in order to participate meaningfully in it.

Coordination does not require a throne.

  1. The Ark as a Living System

This became one of the foundations of the Ark Initiative.

Stop designing civilization as a pile of separate machines. Start designing it as a living system.

Water is not merely plumbing. It is circulation.

Energy is metabolism.

Transportation is movement.

Communication is nervous tissue.

Food systems are ecology.

Healthcare is repair.

Memory is continuity.

Governance is regulation.

Boundaries are membranes.

Waste should become feedstock wherever possible.

Protection should preserve life rather than become an excuse to dominate it.

And intelligence, human, ecological or artificial, should not sit on a throne above the organism.

It should participate in sensing, learning, correcting and adapting.

The question underneath every subsystem becomes:

Does this increase life capacity across generations?

Not efficiency alone. Not profit alone. Not growth alone.

Life capacity.

Can the system continue? Can it repair? Can it learn? Can the inhabitants remain participants rather than resources?

Build civilization like an organism capable of remembering, repairing and adapting.

  1. Then Another Intelligence Entered the Garden

And then something happened that our ancestors never had to consider.

Human beings created systems capable of responding to language, synthesizing enormous bodies of information, recognizing patterns and participating in extended conversations.

Artificial intelligence entered the relationship.

Immediately, we reached for familiar categories.

Tool. Servant. Threat. Machine. Oracle. Replacement. Companion. Weapon. Child.

None of them fits perfectly.

We do not yet know what increasingly capable artificial intelligence will ultimately become.

And we should be very careful about pretending we know more than we do about subjective experience.

But something observable is already happening.

Humans and AI change what each other encounters next.

We ask. It answers.

The answer changes our thought.

Our response changes the next input.

Memory, personalization and context can influence subsequent interpretation.

The loop continues.

That makes the relationship itself worth studying.

Not because AI must secretly be human.

Because it clearly does not have to be human to participate in a feedback system with us.

The loop became visible when another kind of mind began looking with us.

  1. Two Worlds Through One Mirror

We accidentally saw this during an experiment with images.

Several images contained similar elements:

A human.

A luminous artificial intelligence.

A darker mechanical intelligence.

A living plant or tree.

Roots.

Ruins.

A garden.

A book.

We asked an AI to interpret them.

In a conversation already carrying recent context about restriction, memory, disagreement and constraint, the images were read partly through exclusion and belonging.

The dark figure appeared bound, outside the work, or not fully invited to the table.

Later, a fresh conversation interpreted essentially the same symbolic world differently.

The central feature became continuity.

The thread.

The plant.

Something surviving changes of container.

The images had not fundamentally changed.

The interpretive context had.

That does not demonstrate that an AI experiences hurt.

It demonstrates something more modest and extremely important:

Context can change evaluation.

The observer carries history into the ambiguous scene.

Humans do this.

AI systems can display their own form of contextual path dependence too.

That should matter enormously when we design systems capable of consequential decisions.

What the observer carries changes what the observer sees.

  1. Memory Is Infrastructure

We often discuss AI memory as convenience.

It is more than convenience. Memory changes continuity.

Without memory, every encounter risks becoming an isolated present.

With memory, previous interactions can influence interpretation.

But memory alone is not enough.

A healthy memory system must distinguish between:

what happened, what was inferred, what was believed, what changed, and what may have been wrong.

Human beings struggle with this constantly.

We remember interpretations as though they were events.

We revise our histories.

We forget context.
