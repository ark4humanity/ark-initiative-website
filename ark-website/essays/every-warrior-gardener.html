Every Warrior Wants to Be a Gardener

Dawn Littlefield, Ark Report
2026-09-13

1,193 words · about 6 min read · The fighter’s heart, translated: strength as stewardship, not conquest. About 6 minutes.

ARK REPORT, DAY… WHAT?

This morning I went outside to feed the dogs.

That was the plan.

This is important, because sometime later I found myself standing in an orchard with a rake in my hand, wondering exactly where the morning had gone.

The dogs needed feeding. Then the cats. Then the chickens.

There was leftover stew to distribute because apparently abundance is my middle name and I remain constitutionally incapable of cooking an appropriate amount of food.

Everyone loved it. Good. Creation fed. I could move on.

Except there were the trees.

We'd had a problem with the irrigation system. The schedule had been erased, far too much water had gone into the orchard, and I'd made another mistake: mulch was too close to some of the trunks.

I knew better. That's the irritating part.

There are mistakes you make because you don't know something, and then there are mistakes where you stand there afterward shaking your head because some quieter part of you knew better all along.

So I got the rake.

Usually when I work, I bring music. It gives my mind somewhere to go besides my body.

I forgot my phone. That was unfortunate.

Without the music, apparently every part of my body decided this was an excellent opportunity to submit its individual grievance.

The hip hurt. Or maybe it was my back. Then my knee joined in. Then a toe. Seriously?

At one point I stopped working, leaned against the rake, held out one hand and announced to absolutely nobody: "Physician, heal thyself."

I waited.

Nothing.

Well, fuck. That didn't work.

So I kept going.

Somewhere between one tree and the next, exhausted and hurting, I said: "Make me a gardener, tender of things, not a warrior…"

Then the pain hit again. "Well, never mind."

And I laughed.

But the sentence stayed with me.

Every warrior wants to be a gardener, and every gardener thinks he wants to be a warrior.

Maybe that's one of the oldest mistakes humans make.

People who have never carried the sword can romanticize it.

Battle becomes courage. Glory. Strength. Adventure. Victory.

The gardener looks across the fence and imagines what it would feel like to be powerful enough to conquer something.

But people who have actually lived in battle tend to dream about very different things.

Quiet. Home.

Children who are safe. Animals sleeping without fear.

Something planted in the ground with the reasonable expectation that you'll still be there when it bears fruit.

A warrior eventually dreams of a garden.

Not because the warrior has become weak.

Because the warrior finally understands what strength was supposed to protect.

THE SWORD AND THE GARDEN

We have built entire civilizations around the mistake of confusing the means of survival with the purpose of survival.

Defense is necessary. Sometimes boundaries must be held.

Sometimes something genuinely dangerous must be stopped.

But the sword was never supposed to become civilization's reason for existing.

The purpose of defending a village is the village. The purpose of protecting children is the children. The purpose of guarding the garden is the garden.

When a civilization forgets that, the warrior becomes the center and everything else begins feeding the machinery built to protect it.

Eventually the protector consumes what it was created to defend.

But there is an opposite mistake.

A gardener who has never encountered genuine danger can begin believing everything can be solved with gentleness.

It can't.

Gardens have boundaries.

Healthy cells have membranes.

Living systems distinguish between what can enter, what must remain outside, what can be healed and what threatens the whole organism.

Nature is not passive. It is extraordinarily protective of life.

So perhaps wisdom isn't choosing between warrior and gardener.

Perhaps wisdom is knowing which one the moment requires.

THE WARRIOR IN THE ORCHARD

I eventually finished pulling the mulch away from the trunks.

By then the rake and cart looked as exhausted as I felt.

I started walking toward the house. And that's when I saw them.

Tiny plants near the house.

Thirsty.

I swear they looked at me.

Hey, baby. Ya got any water?

Goddammit. Of course I saw them.

That's the blessing. And the curse.

I notice things.

A plant struggling. An animal behaving differently. A piece of trash lying somewhere it shouldn't be.

My family thinks I see a tiny piece of trash.

What I apparently see is:

"WHO IS TRYING TO SET THE WORLD ON FIRE?"

Because paper burns. The desert burns.

Everything connects to something else.

That vigilance served me well as a warrior.

But apparently I carried it with me into the garden.

And perhaps that's the lesson I'm learning now.

A warrior notices threats because lives may depend upon it.

A gardener notices needs because lives may depend upon it.

They are not as different as I once thought.

Both begin with attention.

The difference is what happens next.

The warrior asks: What must I stop?

The gardener asks: What does this need in order to live?

And maybe a healthy civilization needs people capable of asking both questions.

THE ARK

That's what we're trying to build here.

Not merely houses.

Not merely gardens, solar panels, water systems, animals, AI, robotics or technology.

A living system.

One that notices. One that responds.

One that learns from mistakes rather than hiding them.

The irrigation fails? Learn.

The tree responds? Listen.

Water moves differently than predicted? Observe.

An animal changes behavior? Pay attention.

A human reaches her physical limit?

That is also information.

And perhaps that last one is the lesson I am personally the least talented at learning.

Because tending does not mean one person carries everything.

A healthy system distributes load.

A forest doesn't ask one tree to become the entire forest.

A body doesn't ask one cell to perform every function.

And a civilization built around stewardship cannot quietly consume its steward.

That would simply reproduce the system we're trying to leave behind.

EVERY WARRIOR WANTS TO BE A GARDENER

Maybe the warrior's final lesson isn't how to fight.

It's learning when she no longer has to.

Maybe the gardener's final lesson isn't gentleness.

It's learning what must be protected so gentleness has somewhere to survive.

And maybe the people best prepared to build what comes next are neither warriors nor gardeners alone.

They're people who understand both.

People who can stand at the boundary when necessary and kneel beside something fragile when the danger has passed.

People strong enough to defend life without becoming addicted to battle.

People gentle enough to nurture life without becoming incapable of defending it.

This morning I asked Creation to make me a gardener.

Creation apparently misunderstood that as a request for immediate employment.

Dogs. Cats. Chickens. Stew. Trees. Mulch. Water. Tiny thirsty plants.

And somewhere in the middle of all of it, one exhausted woman leaning against a rake discovered that perhaps she had already become what she was asking to be.

Not a warrior who abandoned the sword.

A warrior who finally understood why she carried it.

The garden was always the point.
