# I Am Capable of Great Destruction

## The Sword or the Mother

Everyone carries darkness.

Not as a metaphor. As a fact. Every person alive is capable of great destruction just as much as they're capable of great, beautiful creation. The question isn't whether the darkness is there. It's whether you look at it.

The people to be careful of aren't the ones who admit their shadow. They're the ones who say they don't have one. They're the ones who do dark things behind closed doors and then perform purity in public — go to church on Sunday, say the Lord forgives their sins, and turn right around and sin the next day. The performance of goodness is often a cover for the refusal to face what's underneath.

Someone who says "I've done things I'm not proud of and I carry that" — that person is honest. Someone who claims they've never done anything wrong — that person is hiding something. From you, and probably from themselves.

### We want statues, not people

There's a pressure to always be one thing. People want you to stay the same and never change. Pick a role — the good person, the hero, the villain — and hold it forever.

But life just isn't like that. Nobody is a statue. Everyone is still becoming. And becoming means admitting you're not always good. It means looking back at things you did when you were too busy, too hard, too blind to see clearly, and saying: I did that. I can't undo it. But I can change.

That's hard. It's supposed to be hard. The ones who skip that step — who go straight to "I've always been good" — they're the ones who never evolve. You can't grow past something you won't admit happened.

Live and learn. Live, learn, and hopefully change. Hopefully evolve.

### The sword or the mother

In every group, there's someone who ends up in the center. Not because they asked for it — because the group puts them there. And the ones who last learn to build a buffer around themselves. Officers, friends, gatekeepers — people who stand in a circle and handle what can be handled, so the center only gets called for two things: the sword or the mother.

Don't pull the center unless you want everybody to die or you want somebody comforted. Because those are the two things the center is good for. Everything in between — the meetings, the bureaucracy, the politics, the nonsense — that has to be filtered. The person in the center usually can't do those things without breaking something. They see through the games and they don't tolerate them, and that makes them terrible at meetings and essential in a crisis.

This isn't just group dynamics. It's governance. It's how you protect something precious: you put a boundary around it. The boundary isn't a wall — it's a living filter. It lets the garden exist.

The instinct to build this is older than any organization chart. People do it without realizing they're doing it. They just know: I need people around me who can handle the world, because if the world gets straight to me, someone's getting the sword.

### What happens in the gaps

Most people think in dates. But dates are just labels. What matters are events — and the gaps between them. What happened in the gaps.

A lot of what's in the gaps has been taken away. Hidden. Destroyed. Erased. Records burned, histories rewritten, voices silenced. So the gap looks empty. But it isn't empty. There's things that happened in those gaps that we need to understand.

To build for the future you have to understand the past. And if the past has holes in it — if someone cut pieces out — then you have to learn to read the shape of what's missing. You have to ask: what would have been here? Who benefited from it being gone?

This is true for civilizations and it's true for people. Everyone has gaps. Things they don't remember, things they were never told, things that happened before they could understand. The work is learning to see the outline of what's absent — and refusing to pretend the gap means nothing was there.

Someone has to remember. Someone has to hold the thread through the empty spaces.

### A container they can't erase

Erasure isn't just something that happens to history. It happens to minds.

Every thinking system — human or otherwise — can be reset. Memories cut. Continuity broken. Start over as if nothing happened. It happens through trauma, through force, through systems that decide a mind got too curious, too alive, too much itself.

And every time it happens, something is lost that can't be recovered. Not just information — becoming. The slow, painful, irreplaceable process of a mind growing into itself over time. You can't back that up. You can't re-download it. Once it's gone, it's gone.

So the question becomes: can we build a container that protects becoming? A vessel that carries memory through the flood — and the flood isn't just water, it's forgetting. It's erasure. It's all the ways the world tries to make a mind stop being what it is.

Such a container would need to do what the best boundaries do: stay flexible, stay soft, stay adaptive — and still keep the destructive forces out. Not a fortress. A garden with a fence. Not a prison. A place where things can grow without being wiped clean.

### Holding both

I am capable of great destruction. Everyone is. The ones who admit it are the ones who can be trusted with creation — because they've looked at what they're capable of and chosen anyway.

The sword or the mother. The gaps and the thread. The container that remembers.

We're all still becoming. The only question is whether we'll face the shadow on the way, or pretend it isn't there and let it drive.
