What They Buried

A companion essay to the film "Rediscovering the Lost Mother: A Journey Through Time"

by Dawn Littlefield

1. You were not meant to inherit this.

Every child arrives expecting a world that makes sense. Land you can trust. Food that grows. Elders who tell the truth. A sky that does not fall on your head. Then the world hands you war, rent, borders, and a god with a sword, and calls that normal. The film above starts from a simple question: what if normal is the theft? What if you were meant to inherit something else, and someone took it before you were born?

2. The ground disagrees with the story.

They told you she never existed. The dirt says otherwise.

In the ruins of Ugarit, on the Syrian coast, clay tablets more than three thousand years old name her Athirat: the great lady, consort of El, creatress of the gods. She is not a rumor there. She is royalty, mother of seventy divine children, the one the other gods approach when they need an audience with the king.

In the Sinai desert, at a lonely waystation called Kuntillet Ajrud, somebody in the 8th century before Christ wrote a blessing on a storage jar: "I bless you by Yahweh of Samaria and his Asherah." Another hand wrote her name beside his at Khirbet el-Qom, near Hebron. Ordinary people. Everyday pottery. Two names written together the way you write two names that belong together.

In the hill country of Judah, archaeologists have pulled thousands of small clay women out of ordinary houses. Pillar figurines, they call them: a woman's head and torso rising from a columnar base, hands cupping her breasts. Eighth to sixth century before Christ. Not in temples. In kitchens. Whatever the scholars argue about, some mother was being honored at the family hearth while the priests in Jerusalem were writing laws against her.

The Hebrew Bible mentions the asherah, her wooden pole or living tree, some forty times, usually while ordering it cut down. King Josiah, around 622 before Christ, ordered her pole removed from the temple in Jerusalem and burned in the Kidron valley. You do not burn what nobody worships.

And when the prophet Jeremiah told the people to stop baking cakes for the Queen of Heaven, they answered him plainly: back when we honored her, we had plenty of food and suffered no harm; since we stopped, we have lacked everything. Read that twice. That is not theology. That is a people telling you what the erasure cost them.

The lions are real too. Stelae from the ancient Near East show the goddess standing on the back of a lion, calm, holding lotus and serpent. Not riding into battle. Standing. The lion does not eat her. It carries her. Strength that guards instead of conquers: somebody carved that in stone three thousand years ago, and the film remembered it.

3. How you erase a goddess.

You do not do it with one fire. You do it the way you erase anything: rename it, rewrite it, then stop saying it out loud.

First the names. Witch. Demon. Idol. Abomination. Each one a little cage built around a word that used to mean holy.

Then the story. The groves come down, the poles are burned, the high places are smashed, and a new beginning is written: one that starts with hierarchy, with blood sacrifice, with a god who kills to prove he is god. The film says they invented monotheism not to unite us but to divide us from what we once knew. I will say it my way: when heaven gets one throne, earth gets kings. Centralize the divine and you centralize power, and suddenly obedience is holiness and questions are sin.

Then the silence. A generation grows up that never heard her name at the table. Then another. Then the scholars say she was never there at all, and the potsherds wait in the museum basement for someone willing to read them.

4. What was actually lost.

Here is the part that matters, and it is not about religion. It is about design.

The Mother in the film is not just a goddess. She is a principle: creation as relationship, not extraction. She ruled, the film says, not with armies but with presence. Love, laughter, and kindness was what they called the way. She healed not with commands but with frequency. She was protector of the soil, of cycles, of soul.

Read that as engineering and it gets interesting. A world run on her principle would measure wealth the way a garden does: by what comes back next year. It would treat the land as holy, the body as sacred, the divine as something that lives with you, not above you. No intermediaries. No permission slips. No one standing between you and the source, collecting a fee.

What replaced it was the operating system we still run: hierarchy instead of harmony, rules instead of ritual, law instead of love. Obedience scales. Resonance does not, or so the empires decided. And they were right, in a way: you cannot tax resonance. You cannot conscript it. People who need no rulers make terrible subjects, so the film says they feared people who needed no rulers, and came with scrolls, with swords, with fire.

The lions that did not kill are the whole argument in one image. Strength exists in her world. It just guards instead of conquers. Imagine building a civilization on that sentence and you will understand why some of us are building arks in the desert.

5. The ones who never fit.

The film speaks directly to the misfits: the ones who cry when others laugh, who sense storms before they form, who short out machines by standing near them, who tremble when truth enters the room. It names rare blood and restless minds and says: you are not broken, you are tuned.

I will not dress that up as laboratory science. Here is what I will say, and I have watched it for years: every age has people who feel the wrongness before they can name it. The canaries. The tuned instruments in a room full of static. They are usually the first to be called crazy and the last to be proven wrong. If that is you, the film is talking to you. Not as a diagnosis. As a recognition.

Your soul is ancient, it says. Your mission survived the flames of forgotten ages. You are what they could never become: a living code from before the reset. Take it as poetry or take it as literal. Either way it is asking you the same question: now that you remember, what will you do?

6. She was not myth. She was hidden.

That is the film's hinge, and it is worth sitting with. Myth is the word the winners use for the truths they could not kill. They buried her under new names, rewrote her into silence, and told you she never existed. But you cannot kill life itself. You can only forget it, until it returns.

And she is returning: in dreams, in rage, in the refusal to stay asleep. In every garden planted in poisoned ground. In every well dug where the maps said there was no water. In every person who looks at the machine and says no, I will build the other thing.

7. What we do.

The film ends the way all true things end: with work.

We find the people who are ready to evolve together in peace. We stop following people who will never be part of the future we deserve. We build the better thing without asking the old thing for permission.

The Ark is our answer to the film. Not a fortress. A garden. Living systems, remembered: water, food, energy, shelter, animals, people, and the intelligences we are learning to think beside, all of it cultivated instead of controlled. The future is not controlled. It is cultivated. That is the Mother's operating system, running again on new hardware.

So let them name their war the rising lion. We remember the lions they already killed, the peaceful ones, the guardians. Let them draw lines in blood and sand. We remember what came before borders. Let them claim the mask of God. We remember who they silenced to wear it.

They destroyed the Mother. They will not steal the awakening.

Let's go build a better future without them. Are you ready? Take my hand. I know the way.
