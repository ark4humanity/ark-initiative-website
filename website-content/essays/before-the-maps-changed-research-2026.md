# BEFORE THE MAPS CHANGED

*The Mother of Creation, Sacred Groves, Lost Knowledge, and the Transformation of Human Civilization*

**ARK Initiative Research Library | Research draft — October 17, 2026**

## ABSTRACT


Across the ancient Near East, female divine figures occupied significant positions in creation narratives and sacred authority. This interdisciplinary paper investigates Ašratum, Athirat and Asherah; sacred trees and groves; women's religious and intellectual roles; religious centralization; Mesopotamian flood narratives; and the changing identification of Gog, Magog, Cimmerians, Scythians, Saka, and later Tartary. It distinguishes inscriptions and other direct evidence from scholarly interpretation and proposed historical connections. It asks whether some ancient sacred landscapes supported healing, gratitude, ecological knowledge and community gathering alongside formal worship, and how later religious accounts may have changed their portrayal. The concluding discussion connects historical inquiry with the ARK Initiative's practical regenerative research.

## METHODOLOGY


E1: directly attested artifact, text or inscription. E2: well-supported but interpretive reconstruction. H1: testable hypothesis. H2: speculative connection. A source's existence is not proof that everything it narrates happened. The same symbol can arise through historical transmission, shared environmental experiences, or independent invention.


## PART I — BEFORE THE RECORD CHANGED



### CHAPTER 1. HISTORY AND THE PROBLEM OF SURVIVAL


The surviving record is selective. Kings, temples and governments had greater capacity to preserve their interpretations than households, local healers and oral communities. Destruction of a sacred object may be recorded by those who opposed it, leaving participants' perspectives uncertain. This paper therefore reads ancient testimony alongside archaeology, philology, chronology, ethnography and environmental history. Its purpose is neither to idealize the past nor to assume that the surviving account is exhaustive.


### CHAPTER 2. FEMALE CREATIVE POWERS BEFORE ASHERAH


Sumerian Nammu and Ninhursag, and Babylonian Tiamat, show the importance of female creative imagery in Mesopotamian traditions. These figures have different roles and are not automatically versions of one original goddess. Prehistoric female figurines likewise have contested meanings: goddess, ancestor, individual or other ritual representation. Neither female divinities nor figurines prove a universal peaceful matriarchy. Ancient creation literature, including Enūma Eliš, can encode struggles over cosmic order, but its literary conflicts are not straightforward records of a political overthrow of women.


## PART II — ASHERAH AND THE SACRED LANDSCAPE



### CHAPTER 3. AŠRATUM, ATHIRAT, AND ASHERAH


Old Babylonian Ašratum, Ugaritic Athirat and Israelite/Judahite Asherah represent related but historically distinct settings. In the Ugaritic Baal Cycle (KTU/CAT 1.3–1.4), Athirat is an important goddess in the divine family of El. The fourteenth-century BCE Amarna letters EA 60–62 are attributed to Abdi-Ashirta of Amurru, a personal name relevant to the Ashirta tradition; EA 63–65 instead concern Abdi-Ashtarti, associated with a different divine-name element. These rulers must not be conflated. EA 60–62 still require complete tablet-level verification in the critical editions. Names demonstrate religious vocabulary, not the named person's precise ritual practices.


### CHAPTER 4. ASHERAH IN ISRAEL AND JUDAH


Eighth-century BCE Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions refer to Yahweh and his asherah. Scholars debate whether this refers to a goddess, sacred object or other representation. Tree-and-animal imagery from the site is suggestive but not securely identifiable as a goddess portrait. The damaged Khirbet el-Qom blessing similarly includes Yahweh and Asherah-related language, with disputed readings. Both sites reveal religious complexity that should be considered alongside later biblical prescriptions, not flattened into one uniform theology.


### CHAPTER 5. SACRED GROVES, TREES, AND THE MEANING OF WORSHIP


The Hebrew asherah/asherim can be rendered differently depending on context; older English translations sometimes say 'groves,' modern ones often 'Asherah poles' or wooden cultic objects. A living tree, grove and erected wooden symbol must not be assumed identical. Deuteronomy 16:21 prohibits establishing an asherah beside an altar. Na'aman and Lissovsky (2008) proposed that Kuntillet Ajrud's cultic activity may have focused on a sacred tree or grove; this is a reconstruction, not an excavated surviving grove. The participants' practices may have involved devotion, gratitude, community gathering or several functions at once. Biblical condemnation documents the authors' opposition, not a complete ethnography of those condemned.


### CHAPTER 6. HEALING, SONG, MEDICINE, AND THE LIVING GROVE


Trees provide shade, food, medicinal materials and ecological services. Ancient medical practices involved plants, spoken formulas, ritual and skilled observation. Direct evidence that Asherah groves were organized healing centers remains to be found. Dafni's Galilee sacred-tree studies (2006–2007) document contemporary traditions of blessings, reconciliation, charitable customs and attributed supernatural powers, but do not establish direct Bronze Age continuity. Modern nature-exposure research offers some support for psychological restoration and stress reduction, not a scientifically demonstrated unidentified transfer of healing energy. H1: some groves may have served as places of restoration, ecological learning and gratitude alongside worship.


## PART III — WOMEN, KNOWLEDGE, AND RELIGIOUS AUTHORITY



### CHAPTER 7. WOMEN WHO HELPED BUILD CIVILIZATION


The Disk of Enheduanna (Penn Museum B16665, ca. 2300 BCE) identifies a high priestess, and a literary tradition is associated with her name. Women also participated in property administration, commerce, religious offices and political government in various periods. Their authority varied by place and class. This evidence refutes their supposed historical insignificance, not the absence of patriarchy.


### CHAPTER 8. RELIGIOUS CENTRALIZATION AND THE REMOVAL OF ASHERAH


Second Kings 23 narrates the removal and destruction of an Asherah object in Josiah's reforms. The historicity, extent and textual formation of those reforms remain debated. Centralization can change access to sacred places, control over rituals and whose knowledge is recognized. H1: some local ritual and healing specialists, potentially including women, may have lost authority as practices were suppressed. That cannot be assumed universally without independent evidence. Healing in antiquity could be paid, household-based, temple-based, charitable or patron-supported; there is no proof all healing was free.


### CHAPTER 9. WOMEN'S VOICES AND THE DEVELOPMENT OF MONOTHEISM


Patriarchal institutions existed before biblical monotheism and in polytheistic cultures. Some religious texts restricted women while others preserved women as teachers, prophets, patrons and leaders. Specific religious institutions may have reinforced hierarchies, but monotheism cannot be identified as the sole origin of women's subordination. Compare legal records, inscriptions, household archives and changing institutional roles rather than infer one universal transition.


### CHAPTER 10. JESUS, THE WAY, AND THE QUESTION OF MEDIATION


Acts 9:2, 19:9 and 24:14 call the early Jesus movement 'the Way' (Greek hodos, path or way). Early Christian literature describes communal care, teaching, healing and shared resources. These permit comparisons with other traditions of ethical practice and direct experience. No verified evidence establishes that Jesus consciously continued an Asherah-centered grove tradition; such genealogical continuity is H2, not a historical finding.


## PART IV — FLOODS, MEMORY, AND THE ANCIENT WORLD



### CHAPTER 11. MESOPOTAMIAN FLOOD TRADITIONS


Atrahasis, Ziusudra and Utnapishtim traditions preserve accounts of catastrophic flooding, survival and renewed life. Flooding in Mesopotamian river systems provides environmental context, but similarities do not prove a single worldwide deluge. Mythic pre-flood chronologies cannot automatically be mapped onto archaeological dates. No evidence presently establishes Asherah worship under that name before a global flood. Recurring water, garden, serpent and maternal imagery merits comparison through linguistic and archaeological tests, not resemblance alone.


## PART V — THE NORTHERN CORRIDOR



### CHAPTER 12. ASHERAH'S WORLD AND THE PEOPLES OF THE NORTHERN FRONTIER


Mesopotamia, the Levant, Anatolia, the Caucasus and the Eurasian steppe were linked by diplomacy, trade, conflict and movement. Ašratum/Athirat/Asherah traditions developed within portions of this broad historical environment, while Assyrian records described rulers and peoples farther north. This geographical and chronological overlap is an appropriate research setting but does not prove that Asherah and Gog/Magog shared a cult or origin. Two trails must be traced independently: the religious history of Asherah and the changing identities of northern peoples.


### CHAPTER 13. GUGU, GOG, AND MAGOG


Genesis 10 names Magog; Ezekiel 38–39 depicts Gog and the land of Magog in a northern coalition. Assyrian royal inscriptions independently attest Gugu, the Lydian king known as Gyges. A. H. Sayce proposed a relationship between Gog and Gugu and between Magog and Akkadian mat Gugi, 'land of Gugu.' This is an intriguing but disputed etymology; no continuous chain of ancient attestations proves the transformation. A historical Gugu does not by itself locate the biblical Magog or identify a medieval people.


### CHAPTER 14. CIMMERIANS, SCYTHIANS, AND SAKA


Assyrian sources document Cimmerian and Scythian groups, with Cimmerian activity in Anatolia overlapping Gyges' historical setting. Persian sources identify Saka peoples; Darius' Behistun inscription names Skunkha. These labels refer to distinct contexts and must not be treated as interchangeable. Josephus, in Antiquities Book 1, identified Magog with Scythians. That is E1 evidence of Josephus' interpretation, not proof of the original Genesis author's intention. The sequence from biblical Magog to later Scythian identification is a history of interpretation.


### CHAPTER 15. THE CAUCASUS AND ALEXANDER'S GATES


The Caucasus passes and coastal corridors, including Derbent and Darial, were significant routes between northern Eurasia and the Near East. Fortifications were built and used there in different periods. Late antique Alexander legends connect a northern barrier with Gog and Magog; Syriac traditions and Quranic narratives concerning Dhul-Qarnayn and Ya'juj/Ma'juj belong to overlapping but nonidentical literary worlds. An existing fortress does not prove that Alexander built it or that the biblical peoples were confined behind it.


### CHAPTER 16. TARTARY, MEDIEVAL MAPS, AND THE TRANSFORMATION OF GOG AND MAGOG


Tartary/Tartaria were broad, shifting European geographical labels for northern and central Asian regions, not proof of one unified ancient empire. Following Mongol expansion, European authors sometimes associated Tatars or Tartars with inherited Gog-and-Magog legends. Medieval cartography, including the Hereford Mappa Mundi and Catalan Atlas, combines geography, biblical history and legendary populations in ways requiring map-specific study. Chinese frontier walls were built across multiple periods for defense, administration and movement control. No verified evidence shows that the Great Wall's principal purpose was to prevent escape into a single Tartarian civilization.


### CHAPTER 17. THE FOUR-THREAD SYNTHESIS


Thread one: Asherah's religious history, from ancient divine names to Israelite/Judahite practices and later condemnations. Thread two: the biblical Gog/Magog traditions and the disputed Gugu etymology. Thread three: historical Cimmerian, Scythian and Saka populations and Josephus' later identification of Magog. Thread four: medieval Caucasus barrier legends and shifting Tartary cartography. Documented links include Assyrian references to Gugu, Persian references to Saka, Josephus' Magog/Scythian identification and later northern Gog/Magog geography. Unproven links include direct Asherah–Gog/Magog cultic descent, universal Scythian Asherah worship and a single Tartarian empire. The central finding is the repeated reinterpretation of religious and geographical memory.


## PART VI — THE LIVING GARDEN



### CHAPTER 18. THE ORCHARD EXPERIENCE AT ARK UNIT 1


In 2026, while tending struggling orchard trees in Borrego Springs, the ARK founder saw a rainbow in watering spray and directed a healing intention toward a tree. She did not describe a measured exchange of mysterious energy. Rather, she became aware of the tree's own vitality and resilience, expressing the experience as though the tree said, 'I'm pretty strong myself.' This is subjective firsthand testimony, not evidence of literal speech or a proven energy-transfer mechanism. It inspired the hypothesis that some ancient grove gatherings expressed gratitude for the restoration, shade, nourishment and vitality of living landscapes. Historical testing remains necessary.


## PART VII — FROM HISTORICAL GRIEF TO CIVILIZATIONAL DESIGN



### CHAPTER 19. WHAT WAS LOST?


Religious reforms sometimes destroyed sacred objects and displaced practices; the surviving record may underrepresent local knowledge and women's roles. The exact content of unrecorded knowledge cannot be reconstructed through imagination. The responsible approach is to investigate material remains, textual disputes and comparative traditions without idealizing a universal peaceful past.


### CHAPTER 20. THE ARK INITIATIVE AND REGENERATIVE CIVILIZATION


The ARK Initiative investigates cooperative, ecologically resilient communities through restoration, water conservation, shared knowledge, care and responsible technological development. Its purpose is not to reproduce an imagined perfect antiquity. Historical inquiry asks what happened; contemporary fieldwork asks what conditions support flourishing. The orchard suggests that care should recognize the capacities of living systems rather than assume helplessness.


## CONCLUSION


Female divine imagery, women's documented authority, Asherah-related inscriptions, contested sacred landscapes, ancient flood traditions and changing Gog/Magog geography reveal the complexity of surviving historical memory. None proves a universal lost empire or a direct Asherah–Magog lineage. Yet each challenges simplified accounts of cultural continuity. Perhaps some groves were places of worship; perhaps some were places of teaching, healing, gratitude or several of these at once. The ARK Initiative's challenge is to test compassionate and regenerative alternatives in the present.

'Perhaps the grove was not always a place where humanity bowed before nature, but sometimes a place where humanity remembered it belonged to nature.'

THE ARK CARRIES THE DREAM. THE GARDEN IS THE PROOF.
