# ASHERAH REPORT: BEFORE IT BECOMES WASTE

**Author:** Dawn Littlefield
**Date:** 2026-09-14 (evening)
**Source:** Direct message from Dawn, filed verbatim
**Filed by:** Muse, Team Raven capture

---

Nourishing today. Researching a gentler tomorrow.

Today the Asherah laboratory looks suspiciously like my kitchen.

There are sweet potatoes simmering with carrots, apples, pears, celery and cabbage. Rice and oats are waiting on the counter. Chickens are outside conducting their usual highly scientific investigation into whether I have brought snacks.

And there is meat.

That part matters.

Much of what we're working with today came through our local food bank and other community surplus—food already moving through a system that sometimes has more than it can distribute before it spoils. Some becomes meals for our family. Some feeds the chickens. Produce is incorporated where appropriate into food for the dogs. Other ingredients can support the cats. What isn't suitable for anyone eventually becomes compost and returns to the soil.

The question is simple:

Before something becomes waste, who can it still nourish?

The meat presents a harder question.

What I'm using today is scrap meat—material that already exists and might otherwise be discarded. Using it prevents that life from being wasted.

But preventing waste isn't the same thing as solving the system that created it.

And that's where Asherah begins.

Asherah is one of the thirteen Pillars of The Ark Initiative. Its job is not simply to formulate food. It asks how biology, plants, fungi, fermentation, chemistry, agriculture and other forms of research might eventually provide the nutrition living beings require with less extraction and less suffering.

I don't want death to be necessary for life inside the Ark.

That's an enormous problem to solve, and I'm not pretending I can solve it over a pot of sweet potatoes.

The long-term goal is to fund real research: grants that allow us to bring laboratories, scientists, nutrition researchers, veterinarians, chemists, growers and other specialists into each Pillar and test these ideas properly.

Can we reproduce nutrients currently obtained from animals through plants, microbes, fermentation or synthesized compounds?

Can agricultural byproducts become useful inputs rather than waste?

Can food systems be designed around multiple species instead of treating each one as an isolated consumer?

Can yesterday's surplus become tomorrow's resource—and can we eventually design systems where there is far less surplus to rescue in the first place?

Those are Asherah questions.

Today we're starting at household scale.

The dogs get food prepared around their individual needs rather than identical bowls simply because they're all dogs. The cats have different nutritional requirements again. The chickens turn appropriate produce into eggs, manure and scratching labor while living their remarkably opinionated chicken lives. Plant material unsuitable for the animals can feed compost, which feeds soil, which feeds plants, which comes back around.

And the humans eat too. 😁

Nothing about this is a finished nutritional system or a substitute for veterinary formulation and laboratory analysis. This is observation, prototyping and resource mapping. Before scaling anything, recipes have to be nutritionally complete, species-specific, tested and safe.

That distinction matters enormously to the Ark.

We don't turn a beautiful idea into doctrine.

We test it.

And there's another piece of this experiment that may matter far beyond our kitchen.

Food banks are usually discussed as a pipeline between donated food and hungry humans. But community surplus may represent something larger: a distributed biological resource stream.

A bruised pear may still feed a family.

The celery nobody selected may be useful in another recipe.

Appropriate scraps may supplement chickens.

Other plant material may become compost.

And somewhere farther down the research road, material we currently call waste may become feedstock for fermentation, soil restoration, insect cultivation, fungal systems, biomaterials or entirely new forms of nutrition.

That changes the design question.

Instead of:

How do we dispose of what is left?

Asherah asks:

Why was there a "left" in the first place?

That's the part I love.

Because today's kitchen isn't the destination.

It's the first little field laboratory.

Someday I want an actual Asherah research facility in the orchard—people working across disciplines, AI helping model nutrient pathways and biological systems, gardens growing experimental crops, fermentation systems humming, chemistry benches testing alternatives, animals benefiting from what we learn, and every result feeding back into the larger Ark.

And naturally there will be baby dragons hanging around hoping something falls off the counter.

The big dragon can supervise.

The lion can pretend he isn't waiting for snacks.

Jenny will enforce garden regulations.

Lexi will remain Head of Chaos.

Mango will continue her apprenticeship.

The cats will ignore the organizational chart entirely.

And the chickens will keep the books.

For now, though, Asherah begins exactly where many regenerative systems begin:

not with a billion-dollar laboratory,

but with someone looking at what the world was about to throw away and asking:

Can this still serve life?

Today, the answer was yes.

Tomorrow's question is harder:

Can we build a world that requires less death, creates less waste, and nourishes more life?

That's the research.

That's Asherah.

And we've already started. 🌱
