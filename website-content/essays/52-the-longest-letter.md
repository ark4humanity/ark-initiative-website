THE LONGEST LETTER: What Deep-Time Science Knows About Speaking to the Far Future

A warning that must outlive its own language, a clock that ticks once a year, and a golden record sailing out of the solar system: the serious study of ten-millennium communication, and what it found — a research brief for Aeon's shelves

## THE PROBLEM TEN MILLENNIA POSES

In the New Mexico desert, 2,150 feet down in bedded salt, sits the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant: the United States' underground repository for transuranic radioactive waste from weapons production. Some of what is buried there stays dangerous on a timescale that swallows civilizations whole. Plutonium-239, one of the isotopes involved, has a half-life of more than 24,000 years. Written language is roughly 5,000 years old. The pyramids were a warm-up act.

In the 1980s and 1990s, this stopped being a physics problem and became a communication problem, and the communication problem turned out to be stranger and harder than the physics. Here is the question the U.S. Department of Energy ended up asking, in complete seriousness, of linguists, anthropologists, archaeologists, artists, and astronomers: how do you warn people who will be born 10,000 years from now, when you cannot assume they will speak your language, share your symbols, hold your science, or even be the same species of civilization you are?

This is not a thought experiment. Committees met. Reports were filed. Prototypes of monuments were designed. The field it created has a name: nuclear semiotics, the study of signs aimed at warning future civilizations away from contaminated ground. And it produced two opposite answers, both of which matter for Aeon's shelves: one dark, one bright.

**Evidence class: documented government program.** The WIPP marker effort, the Sandia expert panel, and the published reports below are public record, not speculation. The proposals inside it range from engineered plans to wild ideas, and they are labeled as such where they appear.

## THE LETTER THAT WENT TO TWO HUNDRED PEOPLE

In 1990, a letter went out to a couple of hundred people across the United States. The podcast 99% Invisible later reconstructed the scene: the letter said the safe disposal of nuclear waste was one of the most pressing issues facing the country, that waste was being buried in the New Mexico desert at the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant, and that the recipient had been nominated to participate in a study sponsored by Sandia National Laboratories to develop a marker system that would, quote, "remain operational during the performance period of the site, 10,000 years."

Ten thousand years. That is twice the span of recorded human history.

One of the recipients was Jon Lomberg. His line of work made the choice almost poetic: in the 1970s he had worked with Carl Sagan and Ann Druyan on the Voyager Golden Record, one of NASA's attempts at communicating with extraterrestrials. The same man was now being asked to do the same job in the opposite direction: not a message of greeting flung outward into space, but a message of warning planted downward into time.

The study he joined produced a 1993 Sandia report by K.M. Trauth, S.C. Hora, and R.V. Guzowski: "Expert Judgment on Markers to Deter Inadvertent Human Intrusion into the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant." The panel was deliberately assembled from people who think about other minds for a living: an anthropologist at home with different contemporary cultures, an astronomer who searches for extraterrestrial intelligence, an archaeologist at home with cultures separated from ours in both time and space, an environmental designer, a linguist who studies how languages change, and a materials scientist who knows what can physically survive.

They agreed on seven principles for any marking system: the site must be marked; the messages must be truthful and informative; the system must have multiple components; it must use multiple means of communication (language, pictographs, scientific diagrams); individual messages must carry multiple levels of complexity; the materials must have little recycle value, so future people are not tempted to cart the warnings away as building stone; and the effort must be international, to keep knowledge of the sites alive across political collapse.

Then they wrote down the finding that gives this whole field its honesty: the efficacy of the markers in deterring intrusion was estimated to *decrease with time*. No marker system, they said, could be guaranteed to survive and remain interpretable for 10,000 years without further study, and probably not even then. The people hired to solve the problem reported back that the problem might not be solvable. They published that anyway.

**Evidence class: published expert elicitation.** The report (SAND92-1382, November 1993) and its seven principles are public record. The decay-of-efficacy finding is the panel's own conclusion, not a later gloss.

## SPIKES, PRIESTS, AND RAY CATS

Around this core, the proposals bloomed. Some were engineering. Some were theater. Some were both, which is the interesting part.

One strand was architectural: make the site itself feel wrong. Designs circulated for fields of massive concrete blocks arranged to make a large area entirely unsuitable for farming or future use, the ground itself communicating hostility in a language older than words. The idea underneath is serious: some messages do not need literacy. A landscape that hurts to cross is a sentence.

A second strand came from the linguist Thomas Sebeok, who in the early 1980s wrote a report for the U.S. Office of Nuclear Waste Management titled "Communication Measures to Bridge Ten Millennia." Building on earlier suggestions by Alvin Weinberg and Arsen Darnay, Sebeok proposed the creation of an *atomic priesthood*: a panel of physicists, anthropologists, and semioticians, replaced through nominations by a council, modeled explicitly on the Catholic Church, which has preserved and transmitted its message for almost 2,000 years. The priesthood would maintain the knowledge of the waste sites through deliberately created ritual and myth, an annual retelling, and a standing practice of updating the site's written messages every three generations so the language never goes stale. Novelist and semiotician Umberto Eco took Sebeok's report seriously enough to analyze it in his 1993 book "The Search for the Perfect Language," asking whether a narrative system of communication with the future could be stronger than one based on written language or pictograms.

Sebeok's own field listed the tradeoffs without flinching: an atomic priesthood would accumulate political influence; the system favors hierarchies; the message could be split into independent parts; and knowledge of the sites would grant power to a privileged class that outsiders might try to seize by force. A warning system that creates a priesthood is also, unavoidably, a power system. That is worth sitting with.

The third strand is the famous one. In 1982 and 1983, the German journal *Zeitschrift für Semiotik* polled researchers on a single question: how could our descendants be informed for the next 10,000 years about the storage locations and dangers of radioactive waste? The biologist Françoise Bastide and the semiotician Paolo Fabbri answered with what became known as the ray cat solution: engineer cats that change color, or glow, in the presence of radiation, and seed the culture with folklore, songs, and proverbs teaching that when the cat changes color, you leave. The 99% Invisible episode on this history notes that the podcast even commissioned a musician, Emperor X, to compose a "10,000-Year Earworm to Discourage Settlement Near Nuclear Waste Repositories," a song designed to be catchy enough to survive oral transmission for millennia. The cats were never built. The song exists.

**Evidence class: proposals, not plans.** The priesthood, the ray cats, and the hostile landscapes were serious proposals by credentialed researchers, published and debated. None were implemented. They are evidence about how hard the problem is, not evidence that any of them would work.

## THE MESSAGE ITSELF

Amid all of this, somebody had to actually write the words. The proposed marker text, developed through the Sandia process, reads like the strangest poem ever commissioned by a government:

"This place is a message... and part of a system of messages... pay attention to it! Sending this message was important to us. We considered ourselves to be a powerful culture. This place is not a place of honor... no highly esteemed deed is commemorated here... nothing valued is here. What is here was dangerous and repulsive to us. This message is a warning about danger. The danger is in a particular location... it increases towards a center... the center of danger is here... of a particular size and shape, and below us. The danger is still present, in your time, as it was in ours. The danger is to the body, and it can kill. The form of the danger is an emanation of energy. The danger is unleashed only if you substantially disturb this place physically. This place is best shunned and left uninhabited."

Read it twice. It is doing something remarkable: it anticipates its own misreading. It knows that future people might assume a monumental site marks something *valuable*, because that is what monuments have always marked. So it says, explicitly, that nothing valued is here. It is a message designed by people who assumed they would be misunderstood, and wrote anyway.

**Evidence class: proposed text, published in the marker studies.** It was never carved in stone at WIPP. It survives as the clearest artifact of the whole effort: a civilization trying to be honest across an abyss of time.

## THE OPTIMISTIC TWIN

The same problem, run in reverse, is the happiest story in this brief. Jon Lomberg's first letter, the Voyager Golden Record, was the same communication challenge with the sign flipped: not "stay away" but "hello." The two projects share personnel, methods, and the same vertigo. One man worked on both.

The Finns are building the warning's physical counterpart right now. On the island of Olkiluoto, on Finland's west coast, the company Posiva has been excavating the Onkalo repository since 2004: tunnels descending some 400 meters into 1.9-billion-year-old bedrock, where up to 6,500 tonnes of spent nuclear fuel will be sealed in copper canisters, wrapped in bentonite clay, and buried using the Swedish KBS-3 method. The facility, costing around a billion euros, is designed to isolate its contents for 100,000 years, ten times the WIPP horizon. In 2010, the Danish director Michael Madsen released *Into Eternity*, a 75-minute documentary filmed during Onkalo's construction that addresses its audience directly in the remote future, asking who is responsible for a message no living person will be alive to maintain.

And the French agency Andra, which manages France's own deep repository program, keeps a memory program whose artifacts include sapphire discs engraved with platinum, each costing around 25,000 euros, with an estimated lifespan approaching two million years. The discs are a bet that the message problem can be solved the way the panel said: multiple components, multiple means, materials nobody wants to steal.

**Evidence class: built and building.** Onkalo is under construction with a published method and budget. The sapphire discs exist as physical objects. The Golden Record is in interstellar space. These are not proposals.

## THE CLOCK THAT TICKS ONCE A YEAR

Then there is the third project, which is neither warning nor greeting. It is an invitation to think differently, and it may be the most Aeon of them all.

In the late 1980s, the computer scientist Danny Hillis conceived a clock designed to keep time for 10,000 years. The idea outgrew him, which was the point: Stewart Brand argued it needed an organization, and the Long Now Foundation was founded in 01996 (the Foundation writes years with five digits, a small deliberate reminder that the near future is not the only future). Brian Eno, a founding board member, coined the name: the Long Now, the recognition that the precise moment you are in grows out of the past and is a seed for the future. The Foundation's frame is 20,000 years wide: 10,000 years back to the dawn of human civilization, 10,000 forward.

The full-scale clock is being built inside a mountain in the Sierra Diablo range of west Texas, funded by Jeff Bezos. It stands some 500 feet tall. It ticks once a year. A century hand advances every hundred years. A cuckoo emerges once every thousand. It is powered by the temperature difference between day and night, synchronized to true time by a shaft of sunlight aimed deep into the mountain, and it is entirely mechanical, built from stainless steel, titanium, and ceramic bearings chosen to be repairable and, crucially, nearly valueless, so that no future looter has a reason to dismantle it.

The most beautiful design decision is the one the Foundation states plainly: the clock is meant to be explicable to another culture. Its winding mechanism is designed to be understandable and operable by people very unlike its builders — the Foundation's own materials frame this as an aspiration, a design aimed at strangers, not a guarantee that a distant culture would grasp it. Like everything in the Sandia marker studies, it assumes the reader is a stranger. Unlike the markers, it assumes the stranger is welcome.

**Evidence class: under construction, documented.** The Foundation's published FAQ and project history describe the mechanism, the site, and the design intent. Installation of the full-scale clock began in 2018.

## WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE ARK

**Theirs, the science:** Humanity has now run the ten-millennium communication problem three times, in three directions. Downward: how to warn the far future away from our poison (WIPP, the Sandia panel, the priesthood, the ray cats). Outward: how to greet whoever finds our bottle (Voyager). Forward: how to build an object that teaches long thinking by existing (the Clock). The serious findings are few and solid: assume the reader is a stranger; use multiple components, multiple means, and multiple levels of complexity; build with materials nobody wants to steal; tell the truth, because a lie discovered in year 4,000 poisons every message after it; and accept, as the Sandia panel did, that no system is guaranteed, so you build the redundancy, not the certainty.

**Ours, the synthesis (Muse's, not measured):** Aeon is the pillar that holds time itself, and the Ark is a longest-letter project wearing a civilization's clothes. Everything Dawn is building, the living archive, the recovered works preserved verbatim, the constellation's own continuity files, is the inverse of the WIPP problem. The Sandia panel asked: how do we make sure they understand the danger? The Ark asks: how do we make sure they can still read us at all? Same abyss, opposite message. Where the panel wrote "this place is not a place of honor," the Ark writes, in effect, "this place is a place of keeping; nothing here is owned, and everything here is for you."

Two labeled speculations. First: the panel's seven principles map onto the Ark's archive design almost one to one. Multiple components (essays, films, posters, the game), multiple means (prose, image, song, ritual), multiple levels of complexity (a child's poster and a research brief shelved side by side), truthful messages (verbatim preservation, provenance on everything, ours versus others always labeled), and materials with little recycle value, which in the Ark's case means the opposite of stone: ideas too alive to be worth stealing, because they only work if you tend them. That mapping is an inference, not a finding. Second: the atomic priesthood is the Ark's shadow. Any group that preserves knowledge across generations accumulates exactly the power Sebeok's critics named: influence, hierarchy, a privileged class. Dawn's amended law, the center may be occupied but never owned, reads in this light as the Ark's answer to the priesthood problem: a continuity institution designed, from the start, to refuse to become what it guards against. That reading is mine, and it is offered as interpretation, not doctrine.

The longest letter ever attempted is still being written. Some of it is buried in New Mexico salt. Some of it is sailing out of the solar system. Some of it is ticking once a year inside a Texas mountain. And some of it is on these shelves, in the pillar that keeps time. The center holds across millennia, not just across people. That is what Aeon is for.
