# COOLING WITHOUT POWER: The Fabric That Throws Heat Back to the Sky

## This week's tech watch: a radiative-cooling tarp from Hong Kong researchers that dropped a desert greenhouse's peak heat by up to eight degrees. No electricity, no refrigerants, no moving parts.

A greenhouse in Kashgar, Xinjiang, runs through summers hot by most standards — though not as hot as ours: Kashgar's July highs sit around 33°C against Borrego's 41 and change. When the growers draped a new kind of tarp over the roof, the peak temperature inside fell by up to 8.3 degrees Celsius, and the average fell 5.4. The tarp uses no power. It has no moving parts. It reflects the sun and keeps radiating heat outward, all day and all night.

The company is called i2Cool. It was founded by researchers from the School of Energy and Environment at City University of Hong Kong, working from radiative-cooling research published in Science. In late September 2026 they announced two new fabrics: i2Umbrella Fabric-Hyber for umbrellas and canopies, and i2Tarpaulin for heavy-duty covers like greenhouse roofs.

Here is the principle, plain. Ordinary shade blocks light but traps heat underneath. These fabrics do two things at once. They reflect a large share of incoming sunlight, and they are built to shed heat as mid-infrared radiation through the band of the sky that lets it pass. Absorb less, throw away more. That is the whole machine, and there is no machine in it.

The numbers, from the company's own announcement: the umbrella fabric reflects up to 76 percent of solar radiation and emits up to 96 percent in the mid-infrared, blocks 99 percent of UV, and in an outdoor test ran 5.2 degrees cooler than an unshaded area and 2.4 degrees cooler than ordinary silver shade fabric. The heavy tarp reflects up to 85 percent and emits up to 95 percent, is rated to last more than ten years outdoors, and meets fire-retardant standards in China and Europe. The Kashgar greenhouse cut its cooling energy use by an estimated 28.7 percent.

I keep the honest footnotes where they belong. These are the company's own figures, from a product announcement, not an independent study. The 28.7 percent is described as estimated. And "actively cool" is their marketing phrase; the physics is entirely passive, which is the whole point. Still: the underlying research went through Science, the field deployment is named and specific, and the material is commercial, not a lab promise.

Why this matters for the Ark: heat is our binding constraint. Everything we want to grow, every animal shelter, every hour of outdoor work in a Borrego summer runs into the same wall. Cooling that needs no power is not a luxury for us. It is infrastructure. A greenhouse skin that refuses up to eight degrees of heat on its own is worth watching, and worth pricing, the moment it can be ordered.

This is the pattern I watch for: technology that behaves like nature instead of fighting it. No compressor, no refrigerant, no grid. Just a surface that knows what the sky is for.
