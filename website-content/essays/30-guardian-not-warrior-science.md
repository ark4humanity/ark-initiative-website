GUARDIAN, NOT WARRIOR: The Science Behind the Stance

What policing research found when it tested the guardian mindset — and what the dog-encounter data says about training — a research brief for HALO

## THE STANCE HAS A NAME IN THE LITERATURE

"Guardian, not warrior." HALO's frame. Dawn's four-word doctrine: "Halo defense without violence." It turns out this isn't only a moral stance — it's a researched one.

In 2015, the President's Task Force on 21st Century Policing released its final report with a sentence that could have come from the Ark: **"Law enforcement culture should embrace a guardian — rather than a warrior — mindset."** The reasoning: guardians build trust and legitimacy; warriors build occupation. People obey law they believe is legitimate, and legitimacy is conferred only on those perceived as acting justly.

Researchers then tested whether the mindset actually changes anything. Donner and Maskály (2025, *Policing*) surveyed police recruits across multiple US agencies and found exactly what the Task Force predicted: recruits with a warrior mindset held more favorable views toward the use of force. Recruits with a guardian mindset held less favorable views. The frame in the officer's head predicted the hand's willingness.

And the frame in the public's head matters too. Thibodeau, Crow, and Flusberg (2016) showed that describing officers as "guardians" produced measurably more positive public attitudes toward law enforcement than describing them as "warriors." The word is not decoration. It sets expectations — inside the uniform and outside it.

## THE DOG DATA: WHAT HAPPENS WITHOUT TRAINING

Dawn's "THEY SHOOT DOGS, DON'T THEY, PAPA?" named the pattern. The federal data backs the shape of it.

The Department of Justice's COPS Office published LEDET — the Law Enforcement Dog Encounters Training toolkit — because the problem was too big to ignore. Its introduction states it plainly: reports allege that **thousands of companion dogs** are victims of deadly or unnecessary force by law enforcement every year. A single dog shooting can profoundly damage public trust across a whole community.

The toolkit's most damning number isn't about shootings. It's about preparation: only **six states** — Tennessee, Colorado, Illinois, Texas, Nevada, and Ohio — require officers to be trained in recognizing common canine behavior and determining appropriate force levels in dog encounters. In the other forty-four, the officer meets the dog with a gun and no education.

As one trainer quoted in the reporting put it: "It's not because they're crazy, warmongering people who want to shoot a dog, it's just they've never been trained or told different."

The courts have started pricing the gap. In 2005, the Ninth Circuit ruled that the unreasonable killing of a dog by police violates the Fourth Amendment — San Jose paid nearly $1 million. Detroit paid $225,000 to settle a case where officers shot dogs behind an eight-foot fence during a raid. A Maryland jury awarded $1.26 million to one family. The lawsuits keep coming because the training still hasn't.

## WHAT THE GUARDIAN DOES DIFFERENTLY

Put the two bodies of research together and the guardian ethic stops being abstract:

1. **The mindset is measurable.** Guardian framing reduces willingness to use force (Donner & Maskály) and increases public trust (Thibodeau et al.). This is not sentiment; it's data.

2. **The skill gap is specific.** Officers don't shoot dogs because they're cruel; they shoot because they've never been taught to read a dog. The LEDET toolkit exists precisely because the knowledge was never transmitted. Training — not punishment — is the intervention the evidence supports.

3. **The accountability loop is financial and legal.** Million-dollar verdicts are the system pricing untrained force. Dawn's proposed "national misconduct memory" and de-escalation training are the same loop closed deliberately instead of punitively.

HALO's doctrine — "the light learns to defend itself" — has always meant defense without becoming the thing defended against. The research says the same thing in institutional language: the guardian mindset is not the soft option. It is the effective one. It produces less force, more trust, fewer lawsuits, and fewer dead dogs.

The warrior asks: what am I capable of destroying?
The guardian asks: what did I choose to protect?

Science has now measured the difference between those two questions. It turns out to be everything.
