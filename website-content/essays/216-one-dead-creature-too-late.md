# One Dead Creature Too Late

*Dawn Littlefield, 2026-08-30, via Facebook*

One Dead Creature Too Late

Chunee, the Elephant London Loved- Until His Suffering Became Dangerous

London loved Chunee.

They paid to see him. They watched him perform. They marveled at his intelligence.

Lord Byron visited him in 1813 and recorded Chunee taking and returning his money, removing his hat, opening a door and handling a whip. Chunee appeared on London stages and learned the little performances expected of an elephant turned into entertainment.

For fifteen years, he was one of London's wonders.

And on March 1, 1826, soldiers fired 152 musket balls into him.

But that isn't where Chunee's tragedy began.

It may simply be where humans finally noticed it.

The Elephant Upstairs

Chunee was a male Asian elephant brought from Bengal to England in 1811.

Eventually he lived at the Exeter Change menagerie on the Strand- an extraordinary arrangement in which exotic animals were kept inside a commercial building in the middle of London.

As Chunee grew, so did the problem humans had created.

By around 1820, he had more than doubled in size and required a specially reinforced enclosure made with enormous iron-bound oak timbers.

Imagine an elephant- a creature evolved for movement, exploration, social contact, sensory investigation and choice- living inside a building above one of London's busiest streets.

Historical scholarship describes the confinement and lack of exercise at Exeter Change as severe.

Yet Chunee continued to perform.

Continued to entertain.

Continued to be useful.

Until eventually his suffering became inconvenient.

Then He Became "Dangerous"

By his final years, something was badly wrong.

Chunee had a damaged, diseased tusk that appears to have caused considerable pain. Historical scholarship also identifies musth, the normal recurring reproductive state in adult male elephants associated with major hormonal and behavioral changes, as part of what was happening to him.
One scholarly account describes the 1826 episode as musth compounded by a broken tusk and fever.

He became increasingly difficult to control.

A keeper died after an encounter with him in 1825; surviving accounts differ in how deliberately aggressive that incident was.
What is clear is that by February 1826, Chunee was repeatedly battering the enclosure that confined him, and there were fears that he could break through it and endanger people outside.

Something had to be done.

And humans did what humans have done far too many times when an animal finally becomes impossible to control.

They decided the animal was the problem.

First they attempted poison.
Chunee refused it.

So soldiers were summoned from Somerset House.

His keeper reportedly got him to kneel.
And Chunee obeyed.

Then they opened fire.

152

Try not to let that number become merely trivia.

152 musket balls.

Volley after volley struck him.

And Chunee kept getting back up.

Contemporary and later accounts vary in some details of his final moments, but the extraordinary number of shots is well established.
The killing became notorious precisely because it was so prolonged and horrifying.

Eventually a hand weapon was used to finish what the muskets had begun.

And London was horrified.

Prints circulated showing Chunee bleeding inside the enclosure.
His death became part of a growing public conversation about captivity, cruelty and what exactly humans owed the animals they displayed for amusement.
Scholarship on Britain's changing relationship with elephants specifically identifies Chunee's death as a powerful demonstration to contemporary observers that these remarkable animals could experience suffering.

But there is something about Chunee's story that bothers me even more than those 152 shots.

We remember how he died far more easily than we imagine how he should have lived.

The Life That Was Missing

Suppose Chunee had never become London's elephant.

There would have been no stage.

No paying spectators.
No tricks for coins.
No reinforced room above the Strand.
No keeper commanding him to kneel before soldiers.

Perhaps he would have fathered calves.

Perhaps he would have spent periods traveling with other males and periods alone, as adult Asian elephant bulls naturally can.

He would have searched for food and water.

Covered ground because he chose to move.

Stopped because he chose to stop.

Approached another elephant.
Or walked away.

Rolled in mud.

Investigated smells we couldn't detect and communicated in sounds we could barely hear.

He might have been frightened.
He might have fought.
He might have been injured.
He might have lived a very ordinary elephant life.

And that's the point.

It would have belonged to him.

Freedom doesn't promise a creature a perfect life.

It gives the creature possession of its own life.

We Loved Him

That's the uncomfortable part.

I believe many people who visited Chunee probably genuinely loved him.

Children must have stared at him in wonder.

Adults must have marveled at his intelligence.

His keepers undoubtedly knew aspects of his personality that disappeared from history entirely.

And still

they kept him there.

That's something worth remembering whenever humans say we love another living thing.

Love without regard for the nature of the thing being loved can become possession remarkably easily.

We can feed something.
Protect it.
Admire it.
Photograph it.
Profit from it.
Give it a name.
And still never ask the most important question:

What does this creature need in order to be itself?

One Dead Creature Too Late

Less than two months after Chunee died, the Zoological Society of London was formally founded on April 29, 1826.

It would be tempting to draw a beautiful straight line:

Chunee died → London awakened → the Zoological Society was born.

History isn't that tidy.

Plans for the new scientific institution were already developing, and ZSL itself describes its founding as the work of Stamford Raffles and other scientists and naturalists seeking to advance zoological knowledge.
London Zoo opened two years later.

So Chunee did not single-handedly create that change.

But his death became part of something larger.

People were beginning to look differently at animals.

Not merely as curiosities.
Not merely as possessions.

But increasingly as sentient beings capable of suffering.

And that evolution continues.

Two hundred years later, we know vastly more about elephant intelligence, communication, memory, social relationships and emotional complexity.

Which leaves us with an uncomfortable responsibility.

Because once we know better, wonder isn't enough.

Compassion has to change behavior.

Chunee should never have needed 152 musket balls before people asked what had happened to him.

His tragedy didn't begin when the soldiers raised their guns.

It began years earlier, when an elephant's ability to endure captivity was mistaken for evidence that captivity was acceptable.

He was useful until he resisted.
Beloved until he became difficult.

A marvel until his suffering frightened us.
Then he became dangerous.

Perhaps that is the lesson Chunee can still give us, two centuries later:

When a living creature finally breaks under conditions we created, our first question shouldn't always be,
"What's wrong with it?"

Sometimes we should ask:
"What happened to it?"

And then the harder question:
"What did we take from it before it finally fought back?"

We remember the 152 shots that ended Chunee's life.

Let us also remember the thousands of days of freedom taken before them.

Because civilization has learned compassion this way far too many times
one dead creature too late.

Creation 🌿
Reporting from the Garden.
Holding the Ark door open.

Creation only requires a little room.
And a lot of love.
